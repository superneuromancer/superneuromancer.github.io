## Part 1 — The Foundational Mental Model

### Prompts Are Programs, Not Questions

A question asks. A program *specifies*. The single most important shift in prompt engineering is moving from *asking* to *specifying*.

| Asking (weak) | Specifying (strong) |
|---|---|
| "Write me a summary" | "Produce a 3-sentence summary written for a non-technical executive. Lead with the business impact, not the technical mechanism." |
| "Make this better" | "Rewrite this paragraph to: (1) open with the conclusion, (2) cut passive voice, (3) compress to 4 sentences maximum." |
| "Explain quantum entanglement" | "Explain quantum entanglement using only classical analogies. Your target reader understands probability but not linear algebra." |

**The program metaphor is load-bearing.** Programs have: inputs, constraints, execution logic, and output format specifications. Elite prompts define all four.

---

### The Cognitive Activation Model

Every prompt activates a region of the model's latent space. You are not "asking Claude to do X" — you are *navigating to a region of the model's knowledge and behavioral space* where X is most likely to be produced well.

This has a practical implication: **context shapes capability.** The same model, given the same question, will produce meaningfully different outputs depending on:

1. What role/persona has been activated
2. What constraints have been declared
3. What cognitive mode has been primed (analytical, creative, critical, pedagogical)
4. What output structure has been pre-conditioned

You do not change what the model *knows*. You change what it *reaches for*.

## Part 2 — The Twelve Elite Techniques

### Technique 1: Semantic Crystallization

**What it is:** Compressing ambiguous language into precision specifications.

Every adjective that cannot be measured is a failure point. "Make it professional" means nothing. "Use declarative sentences, avoid contractions, and keep paragraphs to three sentences maximum" means something.

**Before:**
> Write a professional email declining the meeting.

**After:**
> Write an email declining a meeting request. Constraints: (1) open by acknowledging the sender's initiative, (2) decline without stating a reason, (3) offer one alternative (async Loom video), (4) close with a forward-looking statement. Tone: warm but conclusive. Length: under 100 words. Do not use the word "unfortunately."

**The rule:** Every instruction should answer the question *"how would I know if this was done correctly?"*

---

### Technique 2: Role Anchoring with Domain Specificity

**What it is:** Activating a persona that is not just a job title but a *specific cognitive vantage point*.

"Act as an expert" is weak. The model already defaults to presenting information authoritatively. What you actually want is to activate a specific *perspective*, *vocabulary set*, and *set of concerns* that belong to that domain.

**Weak:** "You are a marketing expert."

**Strong:** "You are a brand strategist who has spent 15 years working with B2B SaaS companies navigating category creation. You think in terms of positioning triangles and Jobs-to-be-Done theory. You are skeptical of metrics that don't trace to pipeline."

**The rule:** A role is well-specified when it implies what the persona would *notice*, *question*, and *care about* — not just what they know.

---

### Technique 3: Constraint Stratification

**What it is:** Organizing constraints into three tiers — hard (never), soft (prefer), and negative space (avoid).

Most prompts state what they want. Elite prompts also state what they *don't* want, and distinguish between non-negotiable rules and preferences.

```
Hard constraints (never violate):
- Never use the word "utilize"
- Never begin a section with "In conclusion"
- Length must not exceed 400 words

Soft preferences (prefer when possible):
- Active voice over passive
- Concrete examples over abstract principles
- Numbered lists over bullet points for sequential logic

Anti-patterns (explicitly avoid):
- Hedging language ("it could be argued that...")
- Generic advice ("consider your audience")
- Meta-commentary about the task itself
```

**The rule:** State the output's *edges*, not just its center. The model needs to know where the walls are.

---

### Technique 4: Chain-of-Thought Priming (Forced Reasoning)

**What it is:** Inserting explicit metacognitive triggers that force deliberate reasoning before the model generates output.

The default mode of generation is *next-token prediction*. Chain-of-thought prompting hijacks this by making the reasoning *part of the output*, which forces the model to compute intermediate steps.

**Techniques:**

- "Before answering, state your approach in one sentence."
- "Think through the three most important considerations first, then proceed."
- "Identify what information is missing before making any recommendations."
- "Outline the argument structure before writing any prose."
- "State your assumptions explicitly before reasoning from them."

**The rule:** If the task requires reasoning, make the reasoning *visible*. Invisible reasoning produces invisible errors.

---

### Technique 5: Output Schema Pre-Conditioning

**What it is:** Declaring the structure of the output *before* the model generates it, so the model is filling a pre-defined container rather than inventing structure on the fly.

**Without schema:**
> Analyze this business strategy.

**With schema:**
```
Analyze this business strategy using this exact structure:

## Thesis (1 sentence)
## Evidence (3 bullet points max)
## Counterarguments (2 bullet points max)
## Verdict (1 sentence — "viable," "risky," or "flawed" with one-sentence justification)
```

**The rule:** Pre-conditioned structure is not about aesthetics. It's about forcing the model to allocate cognitive effort proportionally to the sections you care about.

---

### Technique 6: Persona × Task Interaction Design

**What it is:** Designing the *interaction between* the role, the task, and the audience — treating these as a triad, not independent variables.

Most people specify role OR task. Elite prompts specify the *relationship* between them.

**Example:**
> You are a senior software engineer reviewing code written by a junior developer. The junior developer is technically competent but tends toward over-engineering. Your review should: identify genuine problems without discouraging them, propose the simplest solution in each case, and ask one clarifying question at the end rather than making assumptions.

The persona (senior engineer), task (code review), and audience (junior dev) are designed *together*. The interaction is specified, not just the parties.

---

### Technique 7: Latent Intent Excavation

**What it is:** Identifying and addressing the *unstated* goal beneath the stated request.

Every prompt has a surface request and a latent intent. Answering only the surface request is technically correct but often unhelpful.

| Surface request | Latent intent |
|---|---|
| "Edit my email" | Make me sound confident and persuasive without losing warmth |
| "Summarize this article" | Tell me whether I need to read this or not |
| "Write a function that does X" | Produce production-ready code, not a demo |
| "Explain machine learning to me" | Give me the minimum context to participate intelligently in ML conversations |

**The rule:** State the latent intent explicitly in the prompt. "The underlying goal is not X but Y. Optimize for Y while delivering X."

---

### Technique 8: Exemplar-Driven Output Shaping (One-Shot / Few-Shot)

**What it is:** Providing concrete examples of acceptable and unacceptable outputs to calibrate the model's output space precisely.

Examples communicate what definitions cannot. The model's mapping from your words to its behavior has noise. A single well-chosen example eliminates more ambiguity than a paragraph of description.

**Structure:**
```
Here is an example of what I want:
[Example A — correct]

Here is an example of what I don't want:
[Example B — incorrect]

The difference: [one sentence on the key distinction]

Now produce: [actual task]
```

**The rule:** One good example outweighs three paragraphs of description.

---

### Technique 9: Metacognitive Checkpointing

**What it is:** Embedding self-evaluation directives within the prompt so the model audits its own output mid-generation.

```
After completing the first draft of each section:
1. Re-read it as a skeptical reader
2. Identify the weakest claim
3. Strengthen or remove it before continuing

Before concluding the document:
- Have all stated constraints been met?
- Is the argument internally consistent?
- Is there anything I'm stating confidently that I'm actually uncertain about?
```

**The rule:** Self-evaluation embedded in the prompt is not a guarantee — but it increases the probability of the model catching its own errors before delivery.

---

### Technique 10: Cognitive Mode Switching

**What it is:** Explicitly declaring which cognitive mode should govern the output — and switching modes intentionally within a single session.

The model has distinct cognitive modes that produce qualitatively different outputs:

| Mode | Activation phrase | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Analytical | "Reason through this systematically" | Logic, debugging, structured thinking |
| Generative | "Generate without filtering" | Brainstorming, creative expansion |
| Critical | "Find every flaw in this argument" | Stress-testing, adversarial review |
| Pedagogical | "Teach this as if to a curious 15-year-old" | Explanation, accessibility |
| Compressive | "Distill this to its irreducible essence" | Summaries, key insights |
| Adversarial | "Steelman the opposing view" | Debate prep, balanced analysis |

**The rule:** Name the mode. "Be creative" is not a mode. "Generate without filtering or judgment — quantity over quality for this step" is.

---

### Technique 11: Iterative Refinement Protocol

**What it is:** Designing prompts not as single shots but as *refinement loops*, where each output becomes the input for the next stage of improvement.

**The protocol:**

```
Step 1 — Generate: Produce a first draft. Optimize for coverage, not quality.
Step 2 — Evaluate: Review the draft against [specific criteria]. List failures only.
Step 3 — Rewrite: Address each failure. Do not reintroduce what was already working.
Step 4 — Compress: Cut 20% of the word count without losing any meaning.
Step 5 — Final pass: Apply [style constraints].
```

**The rule:** Multi-step prompting with explicit criteria at each stage consistently outperforms single-shot prompting for complex tasks.

---

### Technique 12: Semantic Anchoring with Counter-Examples

**What it is:** Using negative examples (what NOT to produce) to sharpen the semantic target more efficiently than positive examples alone.

The space of "not X" is often more precisely bounded than the space of "X." A counter-example *blocks* a failure mode.

```
Do not write this:
"Leveraging synergistic cross-functional alignment to drive stakeholder value..."

Write this instead:
"Three teams working together cut delivery time by 40%."

The rule: Every sentence must pass the "real person test" — would a thoughtful human being say this?
```

**The rule:** For every output quality you want, specify one thing that would violate it. Negative space is underused and extremely powerful.

## Part 3 — The Architecture of an Elite Prompt

An elite prompt has six layers. Not every prompt needs all six — but knowing all six allows you to choose deliberately.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Layer 1: CONTEXT (Who is asking, in what situation)        │
│  Layer 2: PERSONA (What cognitive vantage point to occupy)  │
│  Layer 3: TASK (The explicit instruction)                   │
│  Layer 4: CONSTRAINTS (Hard rules, soft preferences, avoids)│
│  Layer 5: OUTPUT SCHEMA (Structure, format, length)         │
│  Layer 6: EVALUATION CRITERIA (How success is measured)     │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Most prompts contain only Layer 3. The jump from Layer 3 to Layers 3+4+5 accounts for 80% of the quality improvement available.

## Part 4 — Genius Efficiency Patterns

These are high-leverage patterns that multiply output quality with minimal added complexity.

### The Inversion Test
Before finalizing a prompt, ask: *"What is the worst output this prompt would also accept?"* If the worst acceptable output is bad, the prompt is under-constrained.

### The Newspaper Test
Would a thoughtful reader of your output be able to identify what constraints governed it? If not, the constraints are either missing or too generic to have had any effect.

### The Compression Primitive
After getting a good output, prompt: *"Compress this to 30% of its length without losing any of the meaning."* The model's ability to compress is a diagnostic of whether there was filler in the original.

### System + User Separation
When using APIs or system prompts: put *persona, constraints, and output schema* in the system prompt; put *task and context* in the user turn. This prevents role-drift across a long conversation.

### Prompt Chaining for Complex Tasks
Never put a complex, multi-step task in a single prompt if the steps require different cognitive modes. Chain them:

```
Prompt 1 (Generative):   Generate 20 ideas, no filtering
Prompt 2 (Analytical):   Evaluate each idea against [criteria], score 1-5
Prompt 3 (Critical):     For the top 3, find the strongest objection to each
Prompt 4 (Synthetic):    Produce a final recommendation that addresses the objections
```

Each prompt is simple. The intelligence is in the *architecture* of the chain.

### The Meta-Prompt
When you don't know how to prompt for something, prompt for the prompt:
> "I want to achieve [outcome]. What information would you need from me, and what prompt structure would produce the best result for this?"

This is not laziness — it is using the model's self-knowledge about its own capabilities.

### The Constraint Inversion
Sometimes it is faster to specify what you *don't* want than what you do:
> "Write this — but not as a listicle, not with headers, not in a casual tone, and not with any hedging language."

Four negatives can define a tighter output space than twenty positives.

## Part 5 — How to Systematically Improve Any Prompt

Use this diagnostic framework on any underperforming prompt:

### The Seven Questions

1. **Is the task ambiguous?**
   — Add: output schema, concrete constraints, a single exemplar

2. **Is the persona activating the right cognitive mode?**
   — Add: specific domain expertise + named concerns + named frameworks

3. **Is success defined?**
   — Add: explicit evaluation criteria the model can check against

4. **Are failure modes blocked?**
   — Add: anti-patterns and negative exemplars

5. **Is the task too complex for one shot?**
   — Decompose: into a chain of simpler, mode-specific prompts

6. **Is the latent intent stated?**
   — Add: "The underlying goal is [X], not just [surface task]"

7. **Is there a self-evaluation layer?**
   — Add: "Before finalizing, verify [specific criteria] have been met"

### The Improvement Loop

```
1. Run the current prompt. Save the output.
2. Identify the 3 biggest failures (not the 20 minor ones).
3. For each failure, identify which of the 7 questions above it violates.
4. Add the minimum constraint to address it.
5. Re-run. Compare.
6. Repeat until the worst acceptable output is good enough.
```

The rule: **fix one failure mode at a time.** Adding five constraints simultaneously makes it impossible to know which one worked.

## Part 6 — The Recursive Self-Improvement Principle

The deepest insight in this document is its own method of construction.

**Prompts improve prompts.** A mediocre prompt about improving prompts, iterated through the six-layer architecture three times, produces something qualitatively different from the original. Each iteration:

- Surfaces latent intent that wasn't visible in the prior version
- Adds constraints that the prior version couldn't generate
- Activates a cognitive mode more appropriate to the task

This is not a trick. It is a compression of the general principle that **the quality of your question determines the ceiling of the answer.** The ceiling can be raised recursively — but only if each iteration applies a named, specific operation, not a vague amplification.

"EVEN EVEN FURTHER" is not a technique. "Apply constraint stratification, then compress by 30%, then add a metacognitive checkpoint" is.
